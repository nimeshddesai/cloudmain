Before cloud computing, businesses that wanted to open an online store or let customers book appointments online often had to begin by buying servers. Their teams had to set them up, configure networks, and arrange backups. They also had to estimate how much capacity they would need months or years ahead. Much of that investment had to happen before the first customer could use the service.

Cloud computing changed that. Providers began offering infrastructure as a service on a pay-as-you-go basis, so businesses could access computing power and storage when they needed it and add capacity as demand grew. A promising idea could move forward without waiting for the next round of hardware procurement. That work moved to the providers’ data centers.

For business owners and leaders, the appeal was clear. Providers took over the physical hardware and data centers and, over time, more of the layers above them. More time and resources could go toward improving services, and it became easier to reach customers in ways that had once been expensive or difficult. Patients could book appointments through a portal, policyholders could submit claims from home, and people could bank and shop on their phones.

As more workloads moved to the cloud, providers expanded their range of managed services to support them—from databases, storage, and networking to identity management, analytics, and AI. Applications increasingly relied on these services working together, and the cloud service stack grew more complex. Alongside the business logic behind their applications, teams now had more service connections to maintain and more decisions to make about security, reliability, cost, and compliance.

Today, many teams are once again devoting more time and resources to managing infrastructure—this time in the cloud. The daily work of keeping it running leaves less time for improvements customers would notice: helping patients see a doctor sooner, getting claim payments to policyholders faster, or making checkout quicker for shoppers.
